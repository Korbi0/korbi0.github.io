## Sleeping Beauty Should believe that the probability of 'heads' is 1/2, but her credence should be 1/3

Throughout this, I am working on the presupposition that David Lewis is right and that *there is no reason one could cite* in favour of the proposition "The probability of 'heads' is 1/3", since before the experiment begins, you are convinced that the probability is 1/2, knowing already that you are going to receive the evidence "I have been woken up", and you do not at any point receive any other evidence.

The backdrop of my thoughts is a Sellarsian methodological behaviourism about beliefs, extended to credences along the lines of John MacFarlane's recent paper "Belief: What is it good for?"

I wrote a summary of the way I understand Sellars' and MacFarlane's thoughts in my term paper for Ali's part of PH501, so I am attaching that paper here. The relevant sections are 3 and 4 (in particular section 4.2, which talks about the MacFarlane paper).

The basic idea is, though, to treat "belief" and "credence" as terms referring to objects (internal states) of which we have no observational knowledge, but which are introduced in our psychological theorising to explain certain observed phenomena.

For "belief", the observed phenomenon to be explained is that sometimes people do not show any overt verbal behaviour---they are not "thinking out loud"---but still they seem to have dispositions to make correct assertions and even inferentially extend the set of true assertions they are capable of making. (Note: this is not what Sellars identifies as the phenomenon to be explained here; he speaks more broadly of 'behaving intelligently'. But it is MacFarlane's point that this intelligent behaviour should further be analysed into reasoning behaviour and decision behaviour).

For "credence", the observed phenomenon to be explained is that sometimes people (and other entities) act in accordance with what we might call expected utility maximization.

You should ascribe belief that $p$ to someone else or to yourself iff they are disposed to, in the right circumstances, assert $p$.

You should ascribe credence $c$ in $p$  to someone else or to yourself iff they are disposed to enter into a bet on $p$ at odds $\frac{c}{1-c}:1$ (or, more generally, if they behave in the ways Bayesian decision theory would recommend for credence $c$).

 Not every belief needs to be asserted, but a belief's natural fruition is overt verbal behaviour in the language game of giving and asking for reasons, the making of an assertion.

A credence's natural fruition is to be used in decision-making (as described by Bayesian decision theory) to maximise one's expected utility.

Both asserting and decision-making are normatively governed activities, but their normativities are different:
The norms governing assertion are things like:
- Only assert $p$ if it is true, and if you are willing to provide reasons that speak in favour of $p$ being true.
- Retract your assertion that $p$ if you are presented with strong reasons for $\neg p$.
- If you asserted $p$ and $p \rightarrow q$, you must also be willing to assert $q$.

The norms of decision-making will (at least) include: "Don't be dutch-bookable"

Here is a brief summary of the differences between belief and credence

|                           | Belief                                                                                                                                           | Credence                |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| **Purpose**               | Reasoning (socially)                                                                                                                             | Decision Making         |
| **Normative Category**    | Reasonableness                                                                                                                                   | Rationality             |
| **Norms**                 | Logical coherence; "revise logically incompatible beliefs", "accept logically entailed beliefs", "provide reasons for your beliefs upon request" | Don't be dutch-bookable |
| **Correctness Condition** | Truth                                                                                                                                            | /                       |

MacFarlane says that "one can be rational without ever reasoning, and without ever regarding anything as a reason. Conversely, reasoning---even good reasoning---does not necessarily make one rational."

His framework thus opens up the possibility for "reasonableness" and "rationality" to come apart. I want to argue that in "Sleeping Beauty", they do come apart particularly drastically:

Norms of reasoning require you to assert "The probability of 'heads' is 1/2"

Norms of rationality require you not to enter into any bets on 'heads' apart from those which would reflect a credence in 'heads' of 1/3 (Hitchcock describes a dutch book against someone who bets in accordance with 1/2).

Therefore, on the above framework, we can say:

You should *believe that* the probability of 'heads' is 1/2. If you don't, you can properly be said to violate the norms of good reasoning (perhaps you can properly be called "unreasonable").

But your *credence in* 'heads' should be 1/3. If it isn't, you can properly be said to violate the norms of rationality (perhaps you can properly be called *irrational*).  


I said in the beginning that I am presupposing that Lewis is right when he says that there is no good reason one could give in favour of "The probability of 'heads' is 1/3", making it (via the first norm of assertion) impossible to reasonably assert it, and, therefore, to reasonably believe it. If Lewis' train of thought is wrong, then that conclusion is wrong as well; it would have to be weakened to "Both 'the probability is 1/3' and 'the probability is 1/2' are reasonable beliefs, but only 1/3 is a rational credence". But this would still serve the point of showing how "belief" and "credence" come apart (and how the principal principle is wrong, I guess).

# 1. The birth of a flourishing correspondence between belief and credence out of the spirit of betting

The point at which MacFarlane leaves us is the following picture:
There are two realms of cognition, both alike in dignity, which need be accounted for by our psychologic theorizing: The realm of credences, which alone rationalize and explain our actions (insofar as they are aptly characterized as "rational" or "irrational" we should maybe add); and the realm of beliefs, which alone enter into reason-for relationships, serve as grounds for reactive attitudes and have truth as their correctness condition.

If nothing connects the two, it is thoroughly inexplicable why we would ever even care about beliefs, since they would not have any impact on our actions (insofar as they are governed by decision theory).

Now I want to claim that the practice of betting, which I assume to be an antecedently established practice in society, gives us an opening:
In entering into a bet, you have to anticipate the process by which the bet is to be settled. Assume for now that what I say about the role belief plays in this is correct (i.e. that it is accepted that the bet should be settled in favour of the person who bet on $p$ iff it is the case that $p$, and that the bet will be settled in favour of that person iff either both parties to the bet, or the relevant 3rd party judge, *believes* that $p$). Once (and only if!) you accept that deliberations about whether to enter into a bet need to consider that the payout of the bet depends on what will be true (in the sense of: appropriately be judged to be true by the standards of the society's normativity, i.e.: appropriately be believed), Dutch Book arguments should start to have a bite on you as practical reasons for (not) having certain sets of credences. 

The reason I should not enter into a set of bets which I recognize would require of me to forfeit my money both if $p$ is true and if $\neg p$ is true is that I in general have reason not to give away my money for nothing and I have reason to believe that either $p$ or $\neg p$ will appropriately be judged to be true. 

Once I therefore in general recognize Dutch Books as reasons (not) to have certain credences, the existence of a Dutch Book against someone who believes $p$ but does not conditionalize on $p$ gives me reason to accept conditionalization as a way in which beliefs affect credences. Conditionalization will thereafter be a valid in-channel from the realm of beliefs to the realm of credences. 


(Writing it like that I do feel again that all this only makes sense if we make the move sketched in my previous write up, namely endorsing a primacy of practical reasoning after all. The picture painted above looks like a reduction of the normativity of credences (don't be Dutch-Bookable) to the normativity of believing plus the normativity of betting.)

(Is this a position that already exists in the literature? That subjective bayesianism is what characterizes rationality, but that it can be fully reduced to a belief-based epistemology?)

I guess an interesting feature of the approach would still be that the status of such connecting principles as the principal principle and conditionalization is dependent on the extent to which they really let us avoid dutch books, which can make them fail in scenarios like Sleeping Beauty. 
# 2. Wouldn't a "credences all the way down" theory be better?
## What I take the credence theorist's challenge to be

C: You say I know that a bet is to be settled in favour of the party who bet on $p$ iff $p$ turns out to be true, and this is where our explanations should stop. Likewise for the norms governing assertions of $p$. But would't it be better if we did find a reductive explanation of such norms in terms of individuals' credences and decision theoretic positions? Just in terms of occams razor and parsimony of theoretical entities etc---positing only one type of propositional attitude instead of two.


Well I guess if we are MacFarlanians, we already are committed to having beliefs as theoretical entities, so why not use them for our description of situations for which they seem like the natural descriptive tool to use? To the extent that their independent existence needs arguing for, I take MacFarlane to have done that. What do I think should we make of MacFarlane's whole project if there is, in fact, a convincing reduction of normative features of e.g. assertion and betting to credence-based decision theory? Well perhaps MacFarlane's arguments, to the extent that we ever did find them convincing, should then make us think that belief was a heuristic after all, though not in the way he criticizes ("To ascribe belief that $p$ to someone is just an imprecise way of ascribing high credence to them"---"This would explain the concept's usefulness in everyday language, but not in scientific theory. Just like we use 'tall' still in everyday language after we have 'centimeter' already, but we don't use 'tall' in scientific theorizing."). The relationship between "belief" and "credence" would then perhaps not so much be like the relationship between "tall" and "centimeter", but more like the relationship between Biology and Chemistry (or, not to mix discipline with object of study, like the relationship between "Organism" and "Molecule"). The way in which credence-based decision theory ends up determining the normativities of e.g. assertion and betting would presumably be complicated enough that for many theorizing purposes, it makes sense to investigate the laws governing belief separately from the laws governing credence.

# I. Introduction

In "Belief: What is it Good for?" MacFarlane argues for a division of theoretical labour between the concepts of "belief" and "credence" along the following lines:
The job of "belief" is not, as (traditional / Davidsonian) action theory would have it, together with "goals"/"desires" to rationalize and thereby explain an agent's actions. "Credence" (together with "utility" and decision theory) is much better at that job and so should have it. "Belief"'s vocation is not at all "rationalizing and explaining behaviour" (p.3), but rather its role in *reasoning*---that is, the social practice of "producing, assessing, and criticizing reasons". Ascribing beliefs to agents is only relevant insofar as they participate in that social practice. Within that practice, exactly those agents should be said to believe $p$ who treat $p$ as a candidate reason (p.8), which is to say who take $p$ to be a fact (p. 9). 

MacFarlane, in summarizing this division of labour, emphasises the relative independence between rationality and reasoning:
"*Reasoning* is the process of producing, assessing, and criticizing reasons. So understood, reasoning has only a tenuous relation to rationality. *Rationality*, as I understand it, is a matter of the internal coherence of one's attitudes, both synchronically and diachronically. This is what Bayesian epistemology and decision theory attempt to give an account of. One can be rational without ever reasoning, and without ever regarding anything as a reason. Conversely, reasoning---even good reasoning---does not necessarily make one rational."

This of course raises the question---what is reasoning good for? If, as MacFarlane argues, credences does everything better than beliefs for the individual, why should that change once they are part of a community of intelligent creatures?

# II. Reasoning: what is it good for?
## II.1 Partial answer: Coordinating with others

MacFarlane offers a partial answer to the question---he gives an argument for why reasons should be a helpfully coarse grained version of conceptualising the world as a community which needs to rely on acoustic or written signals to transmit information: Beliefs, he says, can be seen as *digital* in contrast to the *analog* distinctions involved in credence and rationality (p.12); and while an analog signal cannot be copied exactly in the presence of noise, a digital signal can (I assume he has something like the Shannon Noisy Channel Coding Theorem in mind here).

But a part of the story is still missing, namely---what good, exactly, does such communication do us, or *how* does it do us any good? MacFarlane repeatedly emphasizes that belief is not in the business of rationalizing and explaining action. But surely we are owed some story of how belief, reasoning, communication and all that impact action. If there were no such story, the whole exercise would seem pointless.

## II.2 The rest of the story
### II.2.1 Practical reasoning in MacFarlane's paper

The way MacFarlane defines what a reason is, and the example he gives, suggests such a story:

"To regard $p$ as a reason for $\varphi$ing is to take the fact that $p$ to speak in favor of $\varphi$ing. So, the reason-for relation has (at least) two relata: a *fact* and an action or attitude ($\varphi$ing) \[...\] For example, the fact that one's jacket is unfashionable may be a reason against wearing it." (8)

This sounds like facts can be reasons for actions.

Likewise does his *PayPal* example, in which it seems that what he would have liked the PayPal customer service to produce was a chain of practical reasons that would explain their action of denying his money transfer.

However, this seems to be directly at odds with the basic thesis of the paper, which was that it is credence's job to rationalize and explain action and credence's job alone---which suggests that *practical reasoning* is precisely what *should never happen*.

I see two ways forward:

#### Primacy of practical reasoning after all?
Either flip the whole thing on its head one more time. Say that, in the end, it *is* practical reasoning that explains actions, while maintaining that beliefs and reasons have no say in whether an action is *rational*. Leave that as the sole purview of credence and decision theory. These will tell an agent whether the contemplated action is rational. And, perhaps, an action's being rational is usually a very strong reason for doing it, so strong that no other reasons need (usually) be considered. 

(But now that I think about it, this is more of a rationalization for MacFarlane's repeated invocation of practical reasoning than a real answer to the above question; since it does seem that communication between agents often does concern evidence that should have an impact on the agent's credences---but we haven't gotten even one step closer to seeing how beliefs impact credences by embedding the rationality calculus in a practical reasoning calculus.)
#### Beliefs affecting credences
Alternatively, I can see two channels through which beliefs affect credences---an in-channel and an out-channel; but with the out-channel playing a conceptually more basic role (if we take conditionalisation to be justified by a diachronic dutch book, which the subjective Bayesian undertones of the first part of MacFarlane's paper suggest he might). With that parenthesis I have now already kind of given away what the in-channel is, and that is: Under suitable circumstances, if, in a social reasoning context, someone tells me $p$, I will update my credences via conditionalising on $p$.
The out-channel is in the practice of betting as the paradigmatic decision theoretic practice which most clearly and directly reveals an agent's credences. What is the relation between betting and beliefs? I argue that the settling of a bet requires a shared attitude of the parties towards the proposition being bet on, and that this needs to be an attitude whose correctness condition is *truth*---it is part of the normativity of the practice of betting that the stake should be awarded to the party who bet on $p$ *iff* $p$ turned out true. Therefore, the settling of the bet requires the parties to reach agreement on a belief about $p$. 

## II.3 The settling of a bet as a credence-based decision

What about Liam's objection that each of the involved parties might, at the point in time when the bet is to be settled, just make a credence-based decision as to whether it would be prudent to contest the awarding of the stake to the other party?

(To focus on the core issue of the relation between betting and believing, I want, in the following, to assume that the propositions being bet on are of the sort most clearly suitable for betting---i.e. assume that they have clear empirical truth conditions. Have in mind perhaps a proposition like "Liam is going to be in his office on Tuesday at 10am")

What kind of utilities and credence might play a role in such a calculation? What potential costs would you have to consider in your expected utility calculation?

How could you go about not paying the stake when your betting partner thinks you should?
1. You could either refuse to admit that $p$ is true
2. You could concede that $p$ is true but still refuse to pay.

Depending on which of the two options you take, different costs might await you. If you do (1), your betting partner (and any other witness or person they might tell) will stop respecting you as an epistemic agent / will stop taking you seriously. On either option, the other party might resent you or sue you. The latter presumably has a particularly high chance of success if you did (2) and have already admitted that $p$ is true. The courts should rule that if you accepted a bet that would pay the other party \$X if $p$ is true and you believe that $p$ is true then you have to pay the other party their \$X, and they would make sure that the expected utility of complying with the ruling is higher than the expected utility of disobeying.
In that way, the legal system would serve as the explicit enforcing mechanism of the implicit normativity of the practice of betting (namely that if you went short on $p$ and you believe $p$ then you ought to pay the stake).

Now the more interesting case is of course (1). We could distinguish three sub-cases 
1. You genuinely believe $\neg p$
2. you don't have a belief as to whether $p$ or $\neg p$
3. you do believe $p$ but, anticipating that you will want to refuse paying out the stake, make sure to not let anyone now you do

On the picture MacFarlane is painting, there would seem to be a normativity around what one should and shouldn't believe (or am I reading too much Brandom into him here?). He doesn't talk about it too much, but (along, I guess, Brandomian lines) I would assume the norms around belief include things like "If you believe $p$, you ought to be able to give reasons for $p$" (and contrapositively, if you cannot give reasons for $p$, you ought not believe it) or "if you are presented with strong enough reasons for $\neg p$, you ought to stop believing $p$".
For the kinds of propositions we are considering, there will, then, be a clear normatively required belief. It will not be, e.g., acceptable in the relevant sense to believe that Liam is not in his office when we can all clearly see that he is.

If your betting partner believes that you are doing (1.3) (you do believe $p$ but strategically hide that fact), they could appropriately resent you for it. The reactive attitude of resentment is appropriate only if they themselves believe $p$ and they believe that you believe $p$ but refuse to admit it.

If your betting partner believes that you are doing (1.1) or (1.2), they would appropriately stop taking you seriously. This is only an appropriate reaction if they themselves believe $p$ and believe that, given the evidence available to you, no denial or abstention is reasonable.

On either of the sub-cases, they might also decide to sue you. And presumably, in the course of such a trial, the court would themselves evaluate the evidence and evaluate whether $p$ is true. (In which case the legal system would again serve as an explicit enforcing mechanism of the implicit normativity of the practice of betting, that the person who bet on $p$ should be awarded the stake if $p$ is demonstrably true (which for the class of propositions we are considering should coincide with $p$ being true)).


Taking stock: At the very least, in thinking about the potential costs of refusing to pay, you will have to think about whether it is reasonable to not believe $p$ (and, presumably, if the result of that thinking is that it is not, then, according to the normativity of belief, you ought to believe $p$). You will also have to think about whether others (in particular your betting partner and/or the courts) will believe $p$. You will (on MacFarlane's account) think that they will or won't believe $p$ iff there are/aren't good reasons for believing $p$. In thinking about the cost of not paying up, it will be crucial to be aware of the position of $p$ in the space of reasons.

Could a reasoning-refuser double down and say "The disutility of my betting partner resenting me is -10, the disutility of nobody taking me seriously anymore is -100, the disutility of losing a lawsuit is -200, and the utility of not paying my stake is 100. Furthermore, my credences (which I don't have to give any reasons for) happen to be 0.1 that betting partner resents me, 0.01 that nobody takes me seriously anymore, and 0.01 that I lose a lawsuit. Therefore the expected utility of not paying up is 96. It may be that all the reasons speak in favor of $p$ being true, but that doesn't have any effect on my credences whatsoever---after all, it is *belief* whose line of work it is to keep track of reasons, not credence. I will not pay up."?

I guess they could. But that would be stupid.

I think it would be much less stupid to bite the bullet of just following the normativity of the practice of betting. Just pay the stake if you lost the bet. That is how bets are supposed to go and you know it. Even the most subjective bayesians should be fine with that I think. The practice of betting should be the bridge that connects Bayesian epistemology with the rest of life. And any time Bayesians justify Bayesianism via Dutch Book arguments, they implicitly accept this.

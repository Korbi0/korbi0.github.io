Why do we need both „belief“ and „credence“ to describe and explain human behaviour? Because, argues @macfarlaneBeliefWhatIt2025, human beings engage in two kinds of behaviour that cannot be reduced to one another: They have conversations about what they can agree to be true and right, and they decide what is best to do for them individually.
In the realm of decisions which are unaffected by deontological norms, it makes sense to describe individuals as pursuing that course of action which maximises their expected utility, where the utility they assign to a certain outcome is wholly up to them and their expectation is determined by their subjective probability assignments.
On the other hand, certain actions and reactions *are* governed by deontological norms. Reactive attitudes like resenting someone for stealing your phone require (conceptually, according to [[@macfarlaneBeliefWhatIt2025]]; normatively, according to [[@buchakBeliefCredenceNorms2014)]] that you *believe that* they did steal your phone, because (according to @macfarlaneBeliefWhatIt2025), resentment requires a reason.


Kenny [[@easwaran2016DrTruthlove]] proposes a "Belief-first" response to what he calls the "Bayesian view", invoking as motivation for such a response the following five reasons to be skeptical about actual agents actually *having* certain credences @easwaran2016DrTruthlove, pp. 818, 831:
1. It is unclear what exactly credences *are*. @easwaran2016DrTruthlove, p. 817
	- Some say they are dispositions to bet? [[@ramseyTruthProbability1990]], [[@finetti2017TheoryProbability]]; but agents won't always have these dispositions.
	- So some replace this with a normative commitment to find such bets fair [[@christensen1991CleverBookies]], or with the best systematisation of our overall decision-making behaviour [[[@savage1972foundations]].
2. Why should they obey the axioms of probability? [@easwaran2016DrTruthlove, p. 817]
	- Proponents of the betting interpretation give "dutch book" arguments. 
	- Proponents of more general decision-theoretic roles give "representation theorems" arguments [@easwaran2016DrTruthlove, p. 818]
	- Those taking a purely epistemic standpoint [[@joyce1999FoundationsCausal]] see these arguments as more problematic, assuming a notion of "closeness to truth" or uniqueness of what the evidential situation requires.
3. Orthodox Bayesian theory says they are infinitely precise [[@elga2010SubjectiveProbabilities]], but some say this level of precision in the human mind is implausible [[@white2009EvidentialSymmetry]] @easwaran2016DrTruthlove, p. 818.
4. If there is a threshold above which credences act like "belief", then what is the threshold?
5. Actual agents will fall short of the ideal mathematical theory---and if people don't actually satisfy the axioms of probability, their credences will behave differently from what the theory would predict; but then, what use is the theory? @easwaran2016DrTruthlove, p. 818

# The Effect of the Market Price on a Bet on $p$ on Our Doxastic and Credal State

## Rough Argument for: Credences Should (Sometimes) Mirror Market Prices of Bets
The different forms of the efficient market hypothesis provide reasons not to expect to be able to "beat" the market, i.e. not to expect to know better than the market what the value of an asset is --- with the weak form implying that you shouldn't expect to be able to beat the market if all the information you have access to is historical information about the asset's price; the semi-strong form suggesting that unless you have access to non-public information, you should expect to lose money if you bet against the market and the strong form suggesting that you should always expect this. 

Now assume you find yourself in a situation like this, where you do not expect yourself to be able to win a bet against the market, and where the asset in question is a bet on $p$.
If your credence in $p$ was anything other than the market price of the bet on $p$, on the above framework you would be committed to entering into a bet against the market. So, by modus tollens, you do not want your credence to be anything other than the market price of a bet on $p$.

## Rough Argument(s) for: Belief Should Not Usually be Affected by Market Prices of Bets

On the other hand, does the fact that the market price of a bet on $p$ is $c$ have any normative pull on your doxastic state regarding $p$ (i.e. on the question of whether to believe or disbelieve $p$)?

A special case is of course a proposition $p$ which itself talks about, or stands in any logical relationship to, the market price of a bet on it[^1]; in which case the answer is yes. But these seem so weird as to in general be irrelevant.

Here are some arguments for "no, the market price has no normative pull on my believing or disbelieving $p$", based on the two main features of belief [[@macfarlaneBeliefWhatIt2025]] (and I) have been emphasising:
- *Correctness Condition*: The market price comes about by individual's decisions to purchase bets. These are entirely based on these individuals' credences, which, as shown above, do not necessarily have anything to do with the proposition's truth. For example, the market price of a bet on $HEADS$ among participants of the "Sleeping Beauty" experiment would be 1/3; but we agreed that the correct belief about the chance of $HEADS$ cannot be 1/3.
- *Role in Reactive Attitudes*: It doesn't seem to be the case that an arbitrarily high market price of a bet can ever justify a reactive attitude like resentment. Again, considering a hypothetical market whose participants are in the relevant kind of scenario---if, in a large room, everyone has a rationally (arbitrarily) high credence in "Nigel stole the phone", without anyone believing that Nigel did it, because there are no appropriate reasons available for believing this; and if a prediction market was run in this room on "Did Nigel steal the phone?", then the market price would be equal to the median credence (cf. Friedl, Under review at Social Choice and Welfare), which, by stipulation, is arbitrarily high. Yet if anyone formed a belief that Nigel did it on the basis of this price, they would do so without any reason known to anyone that would be suitable to justify such a belief.
- Overall, the market price is problematically inert in reasoning: It does not talk or justify, does not participate in the social practice of reasoning. Where it may be justified to form a belief on expert testimony if one interprets such testimony as a kind of promissory note for reasons available upon request (and expert status as reason to expect these reasons to be good), no such reasons could be requested from the market---because, as the two above scenarios show, it is possible that no such reasons even exist or are known to anyone.


[^1]: Such a $p$ could be a proposition like "The market price of a bet on this proposition at time $t$ will be $c$." or "There is a market for bets on this proposition." But such self-referential markets would be strange enough as to wonder whether, with regard to the rationality of their price, all bets are off anyway.

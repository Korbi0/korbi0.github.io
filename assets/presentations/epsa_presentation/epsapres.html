What is the CJT and What Are Prediction Markets

(Sadly, the fun part of the talk is over now, and we are now entering the drudgery of some very formal results)

General Setting

We have a proposition \(q\)

whose truth value \(\varphi(q)\in\{0,1\}\)

is (as of yet) unknown. We have a population of \(n\) agents

(for simplicity, assume \(n\) is odd),

whose beliefs about \(q\) we denote with \(B_1(q), \ldots, B_n(q)\).

Let \(V(q) \in \{0,1\}\) denote the result of a majority vote taken among the agents.

Assumptions Required for the CJT

  • Competence: Each agent has a better-than-chance probability \(r>\frac{1}{2}\) of getting it right — \(\mathbb{P}(B_i(q) = x \mid \varphi(q)=x)=r>\frac{1}{2}\).
  • Independence: Agents’ beliefs are stochastically (mutually) independent, conditional on the state of the world.
  • Sincerity: Every agent casts their vote in accordance with what they truly believe. I.e. \(V(q)=1\) iff \(\# \{i \mid B_i(q)=1 \} > \# \{i \mid B_i(q) = 0 \}\).

Theorem 1 (Condorcet Jury Theorem)

For each possible truth value of \(q\), \(x \in \{0, 1\}\), the probability of a correct majority vote \(\mathbb{P}(V(q)=x \mid \varphi(q)=x)\)

  • is greater than each individual’s probability of having the correct belief \(\mathbb{P}(B_i(q)=x \mid \varphi(q)=x)=r\) (“non-asymptotic result”), and
  • converges to 1 as the number of participating individuals increases (“asymptotic result”).

Prediction Markets

  • A prediction market on \(q\) is a market trading bets on \(q\) and \(\neg q\).
    • Let \(b_q\) be an asset that pays out €1 if \(q\) and €0 if \(\neg q\) (and vice versa).
    • Let \(p_q\) and \(p_{\neg q}\) denote the equilibrium prices of these assets.

Prediction Markets as Judgment Aggregators

  • Let \(M(q) = \chi(p_q > p_{\neg q})\) denote “the market’s judgment”: It affirms \(q\) iff the market price of a bet on \(q\) is higher than the market price of a bet on \(\neg q\).

Modelling Prediction Markets as Exchange Economies

Definition 1 (Exchange Economy)

An exchange economy is an economy in which there are \(k\) commodities and \(n\) consumers \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\), where each consumer \(i\) is described by

  • a utility function \(U^i\left(x^i_1, \ldots, x^i_k\right)\), assigning a non-negative real number to each possible consumption bundle.
  • an initial endowment of commodities \(\varpi^i = \left(\varpi^i_1, \ldots, \varpi^i_k\right) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^k\).

Definition 2 (Consumption Bundle)

A consumption bundle for consumer \(i\) is a tuple of quantities of the commodities in the economy, \(x^i=\left(x^i_1, \ldots, x^i_k\right) \in \mathbb{R}^k_{\geq 0}\).

Definition 3 (Allocation in an Exchange Economy)

An allocation \(x=\left(x^i, \ldots, x^n\right) \in \left(\mathbb{R}^k_{\geq 0}\right)^n\) assigns a consumption bundle to each consumer.

Definition 4 (Feasibility of an Allocation)

An allocation \(x\) in an exchange economy with initial endowments \(\varpi^1, \ldots, \varpi^n\) is feasible if \[ \forall l \in \{1, \ldots, k\}: \sum_{i=1}^n x^i_l \leq \sum_{i=1}^n \varpi^i_l \]

Definition 5 (Walrasian Equilibrium)

A Walrasian Equilibrium in an exchange economy is a pair \(\left(\bar{x}, \bar{p}\right)\), consisting of an allocation \(\bar{x} = \left(\bar{x}^1, \ldots, \bar{x}^n\right)\) and a price vector \(\bar{p} = \left(\bar{p}_1, \ldots, \bar{p}_k\right)\) such that:

  • \(\bar{x}^i\) maximizes \(U^i\left(x^j\right)\) subject to \(\bar{p}\cdot \bar{x}^i \leq \bar{p} \cdot \varpi^i\)
  • \(\bar{x}\) is feasible.

Jury Theorems for Prediction Markets

Assumptions Required

  • Competence
  • Independence
  • Sincerity
  • Rational Self-Interest: Agent \(i\) purchases bundle \(\bar{x}\) iff \(\bar{x}\) maximizes \(U^i\) among all the bundles that \(i\) can afford.

Further Modelling Assumptions

  • Utility function: \(U^i(x^i_q, x^i_{\neg q})=B_i(q)x^i_q + (1 - B^i(q))x^i_{\neg q}\)
  • Endowment: Let every agent initially be endowed with one \(b_q\) and one \(b_{\neg q}\) (i.e. one euro).

Jury Theorems for Prediction Markets

Theorem 2 (PMJT for Agents with Binary Beliefs)

Under the above assumptions:

For each possible truth value of \(q\), \(x \in \{0, 1\}\), the probability of a correct market judgment \(\mathbb{P}(M(q)=x \mid \varphi(q)=x)\)

  • is greater than each individual’s probability of having the correct belief \(\mathbb{P}(B_i(q)=x \mid \varphi(q)=x)=r\) (“non-asymptotic result”), and
  • converges to 1 as the number of participating individuals increases (“asymptotic result”).

Theorem 3 (PMJT for Agents with Degrees of Belief)

If we let \(B_i(q)\) take values in the interval \([0,1]\) instead of just in the set \(\{0,1\}\), but keep all the other assumptions the same, then:

A Walrasian equilibrium exists, and the market affirms \(q\) iff the median credence is higher than 0.5, and \(\neg q\) otherwise. That is, the market affirms the correct proposition iff the median credence is on the correct side of the 0.5 mark.

Can We Recover the Condorcet Properties in this Case?

Question: How to translate the competence assumption?

We recover both properties if we assume what (Wollesen forthcoming) calls “minimal fidelity of credences”: Agents’ credences are drawn from a distribution on \([0,1]\) whose median is on the correct side of the 0.5 mark.

Corollary 4 (Condorcet-Like Property for PM, Degrees of Belief)

If minimal fidelity of credences is satisfied:

For each possible truth value of \(q\), \(x \in \{0, 1\}\), the probability of a correct market judgment \(\mathbb{P}(M(q)=x \mid \varphi(q)=x)\)

  • is greater than each individual agent’s probability of having a credence on the correct side of 0.5, \(\mathbb{P}(\mid B_i(q) - x\mid < 0.5 \mid \varphi(q)=x)\) and
  • converges to 1 as the number of agents increases.

The Perspective of the “No Trade Theorem”

  • Consider an economy like the one we have been discussing. But assume that agents are risk-averse, have common knowledge of each other’s rationality and of this fact, non-extreme credences, and only accept trades that in (their) expectation don’t decrease their utility. Then no trade takes place.

Conclusion

  • Prediction Markets have similar positive properties to majority voting
  • Unlike majority voting, they don’t have to rely on agents’ sincerity
  • But they have to be paid for in the following sense: Someone needs to enter into not fully rational trades
